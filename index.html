01 The post

On September 11, 2026, a video posted by GMA News started moving fast across Facebook. It showed Vice President Sara Duterte addressing supporters minutes after her arraignment on a grave threats case was postponed by a Quezon City court. In the full clip, she tells the crowd she loves them and can't stay silent because “nakikita ko kayong lumalangoy sa baha” (I see you swimming in floodwater), then turns directly on President Marcos: “umalis ka diyan, sinungaling ka, abusado ka” (get out of there, you're a liar, you're abusive) — calling him “walang utang na loob” (ungrateful) to the people who elected him, saying “wala siyang hiya” (he has no shame), and comparing him to someone on drugs — “ganun talaga pag adik, hindi na dumadaan sa realidad ang pag-iisip” (that's how it is when you're an addict, your thinking no longer passes through reality).

"Utang na loob" is a Filipino value describing a debt of gratitude owed to someone who has helped you. Accusing a public figure of lacking it is a culturally potent character judgment, not a neutral policy critique.

It's a short clip, but it does a lot of rhetorical work. Here's what's actually happening in it, section by section.

02 Truthfulness
Communication ethics

Truthfulness

She really did say this — that part checks out. But the content isn't backed up. She says “nakita ko mismo sa mga mata ko ang abuso ni Marcos” (I saw Marcos's abuse with my own eyes), yet never says what she actually saw. Calling him “sinungaling” (liar) and comparing him to an addict are stated as fact, not as accusations still being proven. And she never mentions her own pending case, which is the reason she was even in court that day. Nothing here points to a document or source the audience could actually go verify.

03 Fairness
Communication ethics

Fairness

A message can be persuasive while still giving the audience only one side.

The clip only gives Duterte's side — Marcos's office gets no chance to respond, and no opposing information or context is included, like what specifically he's accused of. That makes her portrayal of him one-sided by design. And by pairing “abusado” with “corrupt” in the same breath, it nudges people toward treating an accusation as already proven, before any case has been decided.

04 Respect
Communication ethics

Respect and Decency

“Ganun talaga pag adik. Hindi na dumadaan sa realidad ang pag-iisip.”

This moves past criticism into personal insult and doesn't show much respect for the person being described. Calling someone a liar or abusive still points, loosely, at conduct — comparing him to “adik” (an addict) targets his character directly, with nothing behind it but the comparison. It's built to humiliate, and language like this can stir up hostility toward Marcos among her supporters, not just disagreement with him.

Suggesting someone uses drugs, with no evidence offered, is a serious and easily repeatable accusation. It's a common tactic in political speech precisely because it's memorable and hard to walk back — even when unproven.
05 Accountability
Communication ethics

Accountability

This is the clearest gap. According to the post's own livestream link, this speech came right after her arraignment was postponed — but the speech itself never mentions her case, gives no explanation for it, and offers no evidence for what she claims about Marcos either. Instead of addressing her own situation, she redirects entirely onto him, and never accepts any responsibility for what she's facing.

06 Responsibility
Communication ethics

Responsibility

The more shareable a message becomes, the more important context becomes.

Clips like this are built to travel — a punchy phrase, a charged setting, a direct call to action. Content like this can mislead people unfamiliar with the context, stir up anger toward Marcos, and get shared purely on emotional reaction without anyone checking whether the specific claims are true — and that matters, since a message like this can shape public opinion before anything is actually proven.

07 The playbook
Persuasion strategies

The playbook

The clip uses several strategies at once. Tap each one to see why it applies.

Hero/Martyr Framing“Hindi ko magawang tumahimik na lang” (I can't just stay silent) positions her as the one brave enough to speak up, whatever it costs her.
Victim FramingShe frames herself as someone who has been harassed and threatened since 2023, casting her own legal troubles as persecution rather than accountability.

CounterattackRather than responding to her own grave threats case, she turns the spotlight onto Marcos's alleged wrongdoing.
Appeal to AuthorityHer position as Vice President gives extra weight to her claims, even without evidence attached.
FramingShe frames the entire conflict as ordinary Filipinos versus one corrupt leader, encouraging a specific us-vs-him interpretation.

The strongest combination is counterattack paired with ad hominem. Together, they don't just defend Duterte — they change what the story is about and make it personal. That's more than generating sympathy; it actively displaces scrutiny from her own situation onto an attack on Marcos's character.

08 Critical analysis
Reading the message

Critical Analysis

The strongest strategy at work in the clip is a mix of ad hominem attacks and emotional appeal — Duterte leans on personal insults toward Marcos while also tugging at the audience's emotions by mentioning flooding and struggling families. By pairing sympathetic imagery, like Filipinos wading through floodwater, with harsh language against Marcos, she nudges viewers to feel anger toward him and sympathy toward her, without needing to lay out hard evidence. Because of this, the strategy mainly creates sympathy and outrage rather than strengthening the actual argument; calling someone a liar or an addict doesn't prove anything on its own — it's designed to move emotions, not present a case. What's missing from the post is just as telling as what's in it: Marcos's side of the story is completely absent, and so is the fact that Duterte herself is defending against a serious criminal case in that same courtroom, along with any actual evidence for the accusations she's making.

Measured against the principles of truthfulness, fairness, respect, and responsibility, the post only partly holds up. It's truthful in the sense that some background facts, like the election win and the flooding, are real, but the more serious accusations remain unverified. It's not very fair since only one side is shown, and it falls short on respect because of the name-calling. The same concerns, about accountability and alleged abuse of power, could have been raised in a more ethical way, by pointing to specific evidence instead of labels like “adik” or “wala siyang hiya.”

How someone reads this post is likely shaped heavily by where their loyalties already lie. Supporters will probably take her words at face value and see her as brave and honest, focusing on the parts about flooding and struggling families, and reading the harsh language as righteous anger rather than a lack of evidence. Critics, on the other hand, will likely see this as a distraction tactic, focusing on the insults and viewing the whole clip as deflection from the charges she's actually facing, paying less attention to the concerns she raises. If a person supports Duterte, they may evaluate the message more favorably; if they oppose her, they may be more critical of the same message. This shows why personal political beliefs and loyalty can affect how people interpret political communication.

Before accepting any of the claims as true, what's really needed is proof behind the drug-use accusation, specifics on what “abuse” she's referring to, and some kind of independent verification beyond her own statement.

09 Final reflection
Final reflection

Reflection

This activity really showed me how much a message can be shaped by how it's said, not just what is being said. Watching this clip, it was easy to get pulled into the emotion of it — the flooding, the anger, the sense of injustice — before I even stopped to ask whether the actual accusations had any evidence behind them. That's exactly the gap between persuasion and proof. I also learned that being neutral doesn't mean staying silent about problems in a message; it means being able to name a strategy like ad hominem or victim framing without deciding who's "right" or "wrong" as a person. That distinction actually mattered a lot for me personally, because I do agree with the point Duterte was raising about accountability, but I disagree with her approach — the name-calling and insults felt unnecessary and only made the message harder to take seriously, on top of me not really being a fan of hers to begin with. Separating those two reactions was harder than I expected. What stuck with me most is how easy it would be to just reshare a clip like this because it feels true, without checking anything. Going forward, I want to get better at separating my reaction to tone, and to the person saying it, from my judgment of substance — asking what's actually being proven, not just what's being felt. That's the real skill this exercise was trying to build.
