Communication Ethics & Strategy — Case Reading

“Wala Siyang
Hiya”

A close reading of VP Sara Duterte's call for President Marcos to resign — and the strategies doing the persuading underneath it.

GMA News · Facebook · September 11, 2026

On September 11, 2026, a video posted by GMA News started moving fast across Facebook. It showed Vice President Sara Duterte addressing supporters minutes after her arraignment on a grave threats case was postponed by a Quezon City court. In the full clip, she tells the crowd she loves them and can't stay silent because “nakikita ko kayong lumalangoy sa baha” (I see you swimming in floodwater), then turns directly on President Marcos: “umalis ka diyan, sinungaling ka, abusado ka” (get out of there, you're a liar, you're abusive) — calling him “walang utang na loob” (ungrateful) to the people who elected him, saying “wala siyang hiya” (he has no shame), and comparing him to someone on drugs — “ganun talaga pag adik, hindi na dumadaan sa realidad ang pag-iisip” (that's how it is when you're an addict, your thinking no longer passes through reality).

"Utang na loob" is a Filipino value describing a debt of gratitude owed to someone who has helped you. Accusing a public figure of lacking it is a culturally potent character judgment, not a neutral policy critique.

It's a short clip, but it does a lot of rhetorical work. Here's what's actually happening in it, section by section.

Claim
Marcos is a liar, is abusive, is ungrateful to the Filipinos who elected him, has no shame, and — she suggests — thinks like someone using drugs; she calls on the public to keep fighting "the abusive and the corrupt."

Truthfulness

She really did say this — that part checks out. But the content isn't backed up. She says “nakita ko mismo sa mga mata ko ang abuso ni Marcos” (I saw Marcos's abuse with my own eyes), yet never says what she actually saw. Calling him “sinungaling” (liar) and comparing him to an addict are stated as fact, not as accusations still being proven. And she never mentions her own pending case, which is the reason she was even in court that day. Nothing here points to a document or source the audience could actually go verify.

Fairness

The clip only gives Duterte's side — Marcos's office gets no chance to respond, and no opposing information or context is included, like what specifically he's accused of. That makes her portrayal of him one-sided by design. And by pairing “abusado” with “corrupt” in the same breath, it nudges people toward treating an accusation as already proven, before any case has been decided.

Respect and Decency

“Ganun talaga pag adik. Hindi na dumadaan sa realidad ang pag-iisip.”

This moves past criticism into personal insult and doesn't show much respect for the person being described. Calling someone a liar or abusive still points, loosely, at conduct — comparing him to “adik” (an addict) targets his character directly, with nothing behind it but the comparison. It's built to humiliate, and language like this can stir up hostility toward Marcos among her supporters, not just disagreement with him.

Suggesting someone uses drugs, with no evidence offered, is a serious and easily repeatable accusation. It's a common tactic in political speech precisely because it's memorable and hard to walk back — even when unproven.

Accountability

This is the clearest gap. According to the post's own livestream link, this speech came right after her arraignment was postponed — but the speech itself never mentions her case, gives no explanation for it, and offers no evidence for what she claims about Marcos either. Instead of addressing her own situation, she redirects entirely onto him, and never accepts any responsibility for what she's facing.

Responsibility

Clips like this are built to travel — a punchy phrase, a charged setting, a direct call to action. Content like this can mislead people unfamiliar with the context, stir up anger toward Marcos, and get shared purely on emotional reaction without anyone checking whether the specific claims are true — and that matters, since a message like this can shape public opinion before anything is actually proven.

The playbook

The clip uses several strategies at once. Tap each one to see why it applies.

Emotional Appeal She brings up flood victims and struggling families to connect emotionally with viewers before turning to the attack.
Hero/Martyr Framing “Hindi ko magawang tumahimik na lang” (I can't just stay silent) positions her as the one brave enough to speak up, whatever it costs her.
Victim Framing She frames herself as someone who has been harassed and threatened since 2023, casting her own legal troubles as persecution rather than accountability.
Ad Hominem Calling Marcos “sinungaling,” “abusado,” and “adik” attacks his character directly instead of addressing any specific policy or evidence.
Counterattack Rather than responding to her own grave threats case, she turns the spotlight onto Marcos's alleged wrongdoing.
Appeal to Authority Her position as Vice President gives extra weight to her claims, even without evidence attached.
Framing She frames the entire conflict as ordinary Filipinos versus one corrupt leader, encouraging a specific us-vs-him interpretation.

The strongest combination is counterattack paired with ad hominem. Together, they don't just defend Duterte — they change what the story is about and make it personal. That's more than generating sympathy; it actively displaces scrutiny from her own situation onto an attack on Marcos's character.

Critical Analysis

The strategy at work. The strongest strategy at work in the clip is a mix of ad hominem attacks and emotional appeal — Duterte leans on personal insults toward Marcos while also tugging at the audience's emotions by mentioning flooding and struggling families. By pairing sympathetic imagery, like Filipinos wading through floodwater, with harsh language against Marcos, she nudges viewers to feel anger toward him and sympathy toward her, without needing to lay out hard evidence. Because of this, the strategy mainly creates sympathy and outrage rather than strengthening the actual argument; calling someone a liar or an addict doesn't prove anything on its own — it's designed to move emotions, not present a case.

What's missing. What's missing from the post is just as telling as what's in it: Marcos's side of the story is completely absent, and so is the fact that Duterte herself is defending against a serious criminal case in that same courtroom, along with any actual evidence for the accusations she's making.

Does it hold up? Measured against the principles of truthfulness, fairness, respect, and responsibility, the post only partly holds up. It's truthful in the sense that some background facts, like the election win and the flooding, are real, but the more serious accusations remain unverified. It's not very fair since only one side is shown, and it falls short on respect because of the name-calling. The same concerns, about accountability and alleged abuse of power, could have been raised in a more ethical way, by pointing to specific evidence instead of labels like “adik” or “wala siyang hiya.”

Where loyalty gets in the way. How someone reads this post is likely shaped heavily by where their loyalties already lie. Supporters will probably take her words at face value and see her as brave and honest, focusing on the parts about flooding and struggling families, and reading the harsh language as righteous anger rather than a lack of evidence. Critics, on the other hand, will likely see this as a distraction tactic, focusing on the insults and viewing the whole clip as deflection from the charges she's actually facing, paying less attention to the concerns she raises.

What it would take. Before accepting any of the claims as true, what's really needed is proof behind the drug-use accusation, specifics on what “abuse” she's referring to, and some kind of independent verification beyond her own statement.

Final reflection

This activity showed me that persuasive communication and honest communication aren't the same thing, and it's easy to mistake one for the other when a message is delivered with confidence and emotional force. Watching an accountability moment turn into a personal attack on someone else made me realize how strategies like counterattack and ad hominem aren't just clever rhetoric — they steer public attention away from real questions and onto someone's character instead.

I also learned that fact-checking isn't only about proving something false; it's about noticing what's left out. The post wasn't necessarily lying, but it wasn't telling the whole story either. Going forward, I want to get better at pausing before reacting to political content — asking what's missing, who benefits from the framing, and whether I'm responding to evidence or to emotion. Ethical communication isn't about avoiding criticism of public officials; it's about doing it honestly, fairly, and without dodging accountability yourself.
